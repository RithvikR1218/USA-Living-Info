# What:
An IRA typically refers to a tax-advantaged account designed to help you save for retirement on your own, independent of an employer. That's in contrast to workplace retirement accounts like [[401K]]  which require an employer sponsor. The type also changes based on [[Roth vs Traditonal]]

# Why:
Once you get the full employer match on your 401(k), it is widely recommended to start putting money into your Roth IRA. The reason is that the main alternative for excess savings is a standard taxable brokerage account, where you have to pay taxes on dividends and capital gains every time you rebalance or sell stock. With a Roth IRA, your investments grow completely tax-free, and you won't owe a dime in taxes when taking money out in retirement.

# How:
The rules change drastically based on if the accounts are Roth or traditional

## Traditional:
* **Eligibility:** Pretty much anyone can contribute
* **Tax Benefit:** Contributions may be tax-deductible in the year you make them. However, your ability to deduct contributions phases out at higher income levels if you are covered by a workplace 401(k). 
* **Withdrawals:** You pay ordinary income tax when withdrawing funds in retirement.

### Roth:
* **Eligibility:** You must have earned income, and your Modified Adjusted Gross Income (MAGI) must be below the IRS Roth IRA income limits. (2026 is like $153,000)
* **Tax Benefit:** You contribute after-tax dollars but **all growth and withdrawals in retirement are 100% tax-free**. 
* **Backdoor Method:** If your income is too high to contribute directly to a Roth IRA, you can do the following: 
	1. Make a non-deductible contribution to a Traditional IRA (no income limit). 
	2. Convert those funds into a Roth IRA shortly after. 
	3. *Note:* Watch out for the IRS **Pro-Rata Rule** if you already hold pre-tax balances in any Traditional, SEP, or SIMPLE IRA.
